#1.5#Subscriptions#/#
##1. What is Subscriptions##
A subscription is an arrangement where a customer pays on a recurring basis for a product or service. The customer might pay monthly, quarterly, or yearly, depending on the plan.
​
For our furniture business, a subscription could be a monthly furniture-care service. A customer pays regularly, and in return we provide scheduled maintenance, replacement supplies, or another ongoing service.
​
This is different from a one-time sale. We need to keep track of the customer, the recurring plan, the invoices, and what happens when the customer wants to change, renew, or stop the service.
​
Odoo Subscriptions helps bring recurring sales into the same business system. We can see the subscription and its sales information together, and work with connected apps such as Sales and Invoicing.
​
ᴃᴃSubscriptions ➔ Dashboardᴃᴃ
So here we are on the Subscriptions dashboard. This gives us a starting point for reviewing subscriptions and their status. I can open a subscription to review the customer, products, recurring plan, and related sales information.
​
Notice that Odoo uses familiar sales documents. A subscription is tied to a sales order, so the customer and product information are not isolated in a separate system.
​
##2. Set up a subscription product and plan##
Before we can sell a subscription, we need a product that can be sold on a recurring basis and a recurring plan that describes how often it renews.
​
ᴃᴃSubscriptions ➔ Products ➔ Productsᴃᴃ
Here I can open an existing product or create a new one. The product form is similar to forms used in other Odoo apps. I can enter the product name, sales price, and other usual information. For a subscription product, I need to make sure it is configured as a subscription product and can use a recurring plan.
​
For our example, I’ll use “Furniture Care Plan.” This product represents the service the customer is buying. The recurring plan determines the billing interval. If we want to charge the customer every month, we select a monthly plan.
​
The product and recurring plan answer two different questions. The product tells us what the customer is buying. The recurring plan tells us how often the customer is billed.
​
Now let’s put that into a customer workflow.
​
ᴃᴃSubscriptions ➔ Dashboard ➔ Newᴃᴃ
I’ll create a new subscription quotation for our customer. First, I choose the customer. Then I add the Furniture Care Plan product and select the recurring plan. I can review the quantity, price, and other sales details before saving or sending the quotation.
​
At this point it is still a quotation. It becomes an active subscription after the quotation is confirmed. That distinction is useful: we can prepare the offer and check it before committing it as a sale.
​
When the customer accepts, I confirm the quotation. From there, the subscription is managed through its sales record and recurring billing workflow. I can create and confirm an invoice when it is time to bill the customer.
​
So the basic flow is: set up a subscription product, connect it to a recurring plan, prepare a quotation for the customer, and confirm it when the customer accepts.
​
##Live workflow: sell the monthly Furniture Care Plan##
​
ᴃᴃSubscriptions ➔ Products ➔ Products ➔ Newᴃᴃ
I’m going to build this from the product side first. I’ll enter “Furniture Care Plan,” set it as a service, enter the sales price, and make sure the subscription option is enabled. I’ll save the product. The important check here is that I can distinguish this recurring service from a one-time furniture sale.

Next, I’ll open the recurring plans menu and check that a monthly plan is available. I’ll open it and review its interval and billing details. For this demonstration, I’ll use the monthly plan. That determines the recurring schedule; it does not change what the product itself represents.

Now I’ll create the customer quotation. I’ll choose Bailey Builds as the customer, add Furniture Care Plan, and select the monthly recurring plan. I’ll review the quantity, price, and quotation details, then save. Before I confirm, I’ll pause here and check that the customer and plan are correct.

Then I’ll create an invoice from the sales order, review the invoice, and confirm it. This shows the link between the recurring sale and invoicing.
​
As I demonstrate, I’ll say what changed after each click: the product is available for subscription sales, the quotation is confirmed, and an invoice is created. That makes the workflow easier to follow than just looking at the finished record.
​
##3. Manage changes: renew, close, and upsell##
Subscriptions are ongoing, so we also need to handle what happens after the initial sale.
​
If a customer wants to continue after a subscription period ends, we can renew it. In the Odoo workflow shown in the training material, the sales order needs to have been invoiced before it can be renewed. Once it is eligible, we use the Renew action to create a new quotation for the same customer and product, review it, and confirm it.
​
If a customer no longer wants the service, we can close the subscription and keep its status clear in Odoo. That helps the team distinguish active subscriptions from ones that have ended, and keeps reporting more useful.
​
We can also upsell. Upselling means offering a more premium version of the same kind of product or service. For example, a customer may start with a basic furniture-care plan and later want a premium plan that includes more frequent visits.
​
That is different from cross-selling. Cross-selling means offering a separate complementary product. For example, the customer might also be interested in a home design consultation.
​
In Odoo, the subscription can be updated through a quotation for the change. As with renewals, the subscription needs to be in the appropriate invoiced state for the upsell workflow. We should check its status, explain the change, and make sure the revised offer is correct before confirming it.
​
For a short renewal demo, I’ll open an invoiced subscription, click Renew, and review the new quotation Odoo creates for the same customer. I’ll check the product and recurring plan, then confirm the new quotation to show how the next subscription period is recorded. I’ll describe closing as the other outcome: when a customer ends the service, we close the subscription and verify that its status reflects that change.
​
The main idea is that customer needs can change over time. We can use the subscription record to manage that relationship instead of treating every change as an unrelated sale.
​
##4. Customer self-service and automation##
Another useful area is customer self-service. Depending on how the recurring plan and customer portal are configured, a customer may be able to manage certain parts of a subscription without contacting an employee for every request.
​
That can make the experience easier and reduce repetitive work. Before enabling self-service, we should decide what actions customers are allowed to take and make sure the portal reflects our business policy.
​
We can also use automation rules to reduce repeated manual follow-up. For example, a business may create a follow-up activity for subscriptions with a low satisfaction rating. The rule can target relevant subscriptions and prompt a salesperson or service team member to contact the customer.
​
Automation is helpful when it supports a clear process. We still need to decide what triggers the action, who receives it, and what they should do next.
​
##5. Subscription summary##
To recap: we create a subscription product, connect it to a recurring plan, prepare and confirm a quotation, and manage renewals or changes over time. Self-service and automation can reduce manual work when configured around a clear customer process.
​
Subscriptions are most useful when the company has ongoing products or services and needs a consistent way to manage recurring customer relationships.

Now that we have seen how the customer purchases and maintains an ongoing service, let’s move to what happens when that customer needs support.

#1.5#Helpdesk#/#
​
##1. Organize support with teams and tickets##
Helpdesk gives a company a structured place to receive and manage customer support requests. As the number of customers grows, it becomes harder to keep requests in personal inboxes or remember who is handling each issue. Helpdesk lets us organize tickets into teams, assign ownership, and track progress.
​
ᴃᴃHelpdesk ➔ Dashboardᴃᴃ
Here we can see the Helpdesk teams. Different teams can handle different kinds of requests. For example, we might have a General Support team and a Warranty team. The right structure depends on the size of the company and how work is divided.
​
When I open a team, I can review its tickets and settings. Team members can be assigned to the team so tickets can be routed and handled by appropriate people.
​
A ticket is the record of a customer’s issue or request. It should tell us who the customer is, what they need, who is responsible for the next action, and what stage the issue is in.
​
##2. Create and receive tickets##
A ticket can be created manually when a customer contacts us directly. Odoo can also create tickets from configured channels, such as an email alias or a website contact form. Live Chat can also be used as a channel, though today I’m focusing on the main Helpdesk process.
​
ᴃᴃHelpdesk ➔ Team ➔ Ticketsᴃᴃ
When a ticket comes in, we want to make sure the customer information and issue are clear. Then we assign the ticket to the right team or person and decide what needs to happen next.
​
For the furniture example, imagine a customer writes to say that a chair arrived with a damaged leg. We create a ticket, record the issue, and route it to the team that handles product problems. The request is now visible to the team instead of being lost in an email thread.
​
The channels help customers contact us in the way that works for them, while Helpdesk gives our team one place to manage the resulting work.
​
##Live workflow: receive and assign a support ticket##
​
ᴃᴃHelpdesk ➔ Tickets ➔ Newᴃᴃ
I’ll create a ticket for the damaged chair. I’ll enter a clear title, select the customer, describe the problem, choose the support team, and assign an owner. I’ll save the ticket and point out the fields that help the next person understand what the customer needs.
​
Next, I’ll open the team’s Kanban pipeline. I’ll show the New column and move the ticket to In Progress once someone starts working on it. If we are waiting for a photo or more information from the customer, I’ll move it to a waiting stage. Once the issue is resolved, I’ll move it to the team’s solved or closed stage. I’ll explain that stage names depend on the company’s own process.
​
If I have a demo database ready, I’ll also show one configured intake channel, such as the website contact form or email alias. I’ll explain that incoming requests from these channels can create tickets for the team, so employees can manage them in the same pipeline. I won’t spend time setting up every channel during this presentation.
​
##3. Stages and Service Level Agreements##
Tickets move through stages as the team works on them. For example, we might use stages such as New, In Progress, Waiting for Customer, and Solved. The exact names should match the company’s workflow.
​
ᴃᴃHelpdesk ➔ Team ➔ Ticketsᴃᴃ
In the Kanban view, each ticket appears as a card. As work progresses, we can move the ticket from one stage to another. This gives the team a quick view of what has arrived, what is being worked on, and what has been completed.
​
Stages describe the work process. An SLA, or Service Level Agreement, describes the service target and the time in which we aim to respond or resolve an issue.
​
For example, a company might set a faster target for urgent requests than for general questions. The SLA policy can be associated with the relevant team and tickets. The working-hours calendar matters because service time should be measured against the hours the team is expected to work.
​
ᴃᴃHelpdesk ➔ Configuration ➔ SLA Policiesᴃᴃ
For the demo, I’ll open an existing SLA policy or create a simple one. I’ll choose the Helpdesk team, define which tickets the policy applies to, select the target stage and time limit, then save it. I’ll point out the working-hours calendar used by the team and show how the SLA appears on a matching ticket. The exact conditions depend on the business, so I’ll use a simple example rather than configure a complicated policy live.
​
When we configure an SLA, we need to be clear about the target, which tickets it applies to, and the working hours we use. A target is only useful if the team understands it and the policy reflects what the business can actually deliver.
​
A ticket being in a later stage does not automatically mean the customer is satisfied. The stage tells us where the work is. The SLA tells us whether the service target is being met. Both views help us manage support, but they answer different questions.
​
##4. Customer ratings and after-sales actions##
After a ticket is resolved, customer feedback can help us understand how the support experience went. With customer ratings enabled for the team, customers can provide a rating and may add a comment. The team can review those ratings and look for patterns.
​
If customers repeatedly mention slow responses, we may need to review assignments or workload. If feedback is positive, we can see which parts of the process are working well.
​
Helpdesk can also connect support work to after-sales actions. For our damaged chair, the resolution might involve arranging a return or refund, depending on company policy and the customer’s situation.
​
ᴃᴃHelpdesk ➔ Team Settings ➔ After-Sales Featuresᴃᴃ
When relevant features are enabled for a team, a support employee can start an action such as a return or refund from the ticket workflow. Depending on the action, Odoo can connect the support request to related sales or inventory records. This saves the team from treating the customer issue and the business transaction as completely separate processes.
​
We should choose the right action based on the case. A return is used when the product needs to come back. A refund is used when money needs to be credited to the customer. The employee should review the order and follow the company’s approval and accounting process.
​
##5. Helpdesk summary##
To recap Helpdesk: we organize people into teams, receive tickets through one or more channels, move tickets through stages, use SLAs to set service expectations, and review feedback after an issue is resolved. When an issue concerns a product sale, after-sales actions can connect support work to other Odoo records.
​
That completes the support part of our customer journey. We have attracted a customer, started an ongoing relationship, and handled a support need. Now let’s look at how Marketing helps us communicate with current and potential customers.
​
#1.5#Marketing#/#
​
##1. Plan communications for the right audience##
Marketing helps a business communicate with current customers, potential customers, and other groups. The goal is not simply to send as many messages as possible. We need to send useful information to the right audience and understand how people respond.
​
Odoo Marketing includes tools for email marketing and marketing automation. The subtitles also cover SMS and social marketing, but I’m focusing today on a practical email workflow and a short look at automation because those connect well with the customer journey we have discussed.
​
Before sending a message, we need to decide who should receive it, what we want them to do, and what result would show that the message worked.
​
##2. Mailing lists, templates, and email campaigns##
ᴃᴃEmail Marketing ➔ Mailing Lists ➔ Mailing Listsᴃᴃ
Mailing lists help us organize contacts by audience or purpose. For example, we might have one list for customers who have bought furniture and another for people who signed up to hear about upcoming offers.
​
A well-organized list makes it easier to send relevant messages. We should respect opt-outs and only contact people according to the company’s communication policy. Keeping the list accurate is part of good customer communication.
​
Next, we can prepare the email content. Odoo provides email templates and building blocks that we can adapt to our company’s design. We can add a clear subject, explain the offer, and include one main call to action. Before sending, we should check how the message looks and verify its links and audience.
​
A mailing is one standalone email. A campaign is a series of mailings organized around a broader communication goal. For example, we might send an announcement about our furniture-care subscription, then a follow-up with more details, and later a reminder. That sequence is a campaign; each individual email is a mailing.
​
ᴃᴃEmail Marketing ➔ Mailingsᴃᴃ
For our example, the goal is to introduce the Furniture Care Plan to existing customers. I would first define the audience, then create an email that explains what the plan includes, how often it renews, and where the customer can learn more. The call to action could take the customer to the product or subscription page.
​
If we are using a campaign, we can organize related emails together and plan their timing. We should avoid sending repeated messages without a clear reason. A good campaign has a purpose and a sensible pace.
​
##Live workflow: prepare an email for the right audience##
​
ᴃᴃEmail Marketing ➔ Mailing Lists ➔ Mailing Lists ➔ Newᴃᴃ
I’ll create or open a mailing list for existing furniture customers. I’ll review the contacts on the list and explain that this audience is selected because the offer is relevant to them. I’ll also point out that a customer’s opt-out preference must be respected.
​
ᴃᴃEmail Marketing ➔ Mailings ➔ Newᴃᴃ
Now I’ll create a mailing called “Furniture Care Plan.” I’ll enter a clear subject, select the mailing list, and build the email using the available blocks or a template. I’ll keep the message focused: what the plan includes, how often it renews, and one clear action for the reader. Before sending, I’ll preview the email, check its links, and verify the recipients.
​
If I’m showing a campaign instead of a single mailing, I’ll first enable the mailing campaigns feature in settings if it is not already enabled. Then I’ll create the campaign and add the related mailings. I’ll explain the difference on screen: the campaign organizes a sequence, while each mailing is an individual email.
​
I’ll stop before sending to a live audience unless the database is set up for a real demonstration. The purpose of this walkthrough is to show the preparation and review steps, including checking the audience and message before launch.
​
##3. A/B testing and learning from results##
Sometimes we are unsure which subject line or call to action will work better. A/B testing lets us compare versions of an email with a small portion of the audience.
​
For example, version A might have a subject line focused on convenience, while version B emphasizes the service benefit. We choose a measure such as opens or clicks, and Odoo can use the result to select the more effective version for the remaining recipients.
​
The point is to test one meaningful difference at a time. If we change the subject, image, offer, and call to action all at once, it becomes hard to tell what caused a change in the result.
​
The winning version is better according to the measure we selected. A higher open rate does not always mean more sales, so we should choose a measure that fits our goal and review the result in context.
​
##4. Marketing automation##
Marketing automation helps us send messages or trigger activities based on defined conditions. This can be useful when a business needs to follow up with leads or customers at the right time without manually checking every record.
​
For example, we might create a campaign for people who have shown interest in a product but have not yet purchased. The campaign could send an initial email and then schedule a follow-up activity based on what happens next.
​
ᴃᴃMarketing Automation ➔ Campaigns ➔ Newᴃᴃ
When setting up an automation, we first define the target audience or records. Then we add activities, such as sending an email or creating a follow-up task. We also define the conditions that trigger the next activity.
​
Before launching the campaign, we should test it on a record and confirm that the right audience, content, and activities are selected. The training material demonstrates using a test record before running the campaign. This gives us a chance to catch a configuration mistake before real customers are contacted.
​
Automation should make a good process easier to run. It should not mean that we send messages without considering timing, relevance, or customer preferences. We still need to review the campaign and make sure it is doing what we intended.
​
For the live demo, I’ll open an existing test campaign rather than build a complex automation from scratch. I’ll show the target audience, one email activity, and the condition that controls the next step. Then I’ll use the test option on a sample record and confirm that the expected activity appears. Only after reviewing the test result would I consider launching it.
​
##5. Marketing summary##
To recap Marketing: we organize contacts into relevant mailing lists, create clear email content, use campaigns when we have a sequence of messages, and test variations when we want to compare approaches. Marketing automation can handle repeatable follow-up when we define the audience and conditions carefully.
​
##Closing##
Let’s connect the three modules one last time.
​
Marketing helps us introduce a product or service and communicate with the right audience. When a customer is ready to buy an ongoing service, Subscriptions helps us manage the recurring product, plan, and customer relationship. If the customer has a question or an issue, Helpdesk gives the team a structured process for taking ownership, tracking progress, meeting service targets, and collecting feedback.
​
The three modules support different parts of the same customer relationship. The best results come when the processes are clear and the information is connected across Odoo.
