#1.5#Subscriptions, Helpdesk & Marketing#/#

Hello everyone. Today, I’m going to walk you through three Odoo modules: **Subscriptions**, **Helpdesk**, and **Marketing**.

So lets start with ᴃᴃSubscriptionᴃᴃ

#1.5#Subscriptions#/#

##1. Subscription dashboard##
A subscription is a product or service that a customer pays for on a recurring schedule, like monthly or yearly. I’ll show how we set up that schedule, sell it, and manage it after the sale.

ᴃᴃSubscriptions ➔ Dashboardᴃᴃ
So here we are on the Subscriptions dashboard. I’ll start by pointing out the records and statuses shown here. This gives us a quick view of the subscriptions the company is managing.

I’m going to open one subscription. On this form, I’ll point out the customer, the product, the recurring plan, and the order information. These are the details I need when I want to understand what the customer bought and what happens next.

You can see that the subscription is connected to a sales order. So we can follow the customer’s recurring service through familiar Odoo sales information.

##2. Check the recurring plan and product##
Before we create a subscription for a customer, I’ll check the plan and product we are going to use.

ᴃᴃSubscriptions ➔ Configuration ➔ Recurring Plansᴃᴃ
Here I’ll open the monthly plan. I’ll point to the plan name and recurrence interval. This is where I confirm how often the customer will be billed. For today’s example, we’re using a monthly Furniture Care Plan.

ᴃᴃSubscriptions ➔ Products ➔ Productsᴃᴃ
Now I’ll open the Furniture Care Plan product. I’ll show the product name and sales price, then point out the subscription-related option or recurring details on the form. The product tells us what the customer is buying; the recurring plan tells us how often the order repeats.

I’m not going to spend time on every general product field. I’ll focus on the fields that affect this workflow: the product, the price, and the recurring plan.

##3. Create and confirm the customer quotation##
ᴃᴃSubscriptions ➔ Dashboard ➔ Newᴃᴃ
Now I’ll create a quotation for Bailey Builds. On the quotation, I’ll select the customer first. Then I’ll add the Furniture Care Plan on the order line.

I’ll point out the order line, where we can review the product, quantity, and price. I’ll select the monthly recurring plan and check the quotation details before confirming anything.

At this point, this is still a quotation. I can review it with the customer and make changes before confirming it. Once the customer accepts, I’ll click **Confirm**.

Now we can see that the quotation has become a confirmed sales order and the subscription is active. I’ll pause here so you can see the status and the recurring plan on the record.

Next, I’ll click **Create Invoice**. I’ll review the invoice that Odoo prepares, then confirm it. This shows the next step after the sale: the customer’s recurring service is connected to the invoicing workflow.

So on screen, we just followed the process from product, to quotation, to confirmed subscription, to invoice.

##4. Renew or change a subscription##
Now I’ll show what happens when a customer wants to continue or change the service.

I’ll open an invoiced subscription. In this example, the order has already been invoiced, which is needed for the renewal workflow shown in the training material. I’ll click **Renew** and review the new quotation Odoo creates for the same customer and product.

Before confirming the renewal, I’ll check the recurring plan and order lines again. Then I can confirm the new quotation and show how the next period is recorded.

If the customer wants a higher level of service, we can use an upsell quotation to offer a more premium version. For example, we could move from the basic furniture-care plan to a plan with more frequent visits. I’ll explain that this is an upsell because it improves the same service. A separate home design consultation would be a cross-sell because it is a different, complementary service.

If the customer no longer wants the service, we close the subscription and check that the status reflects that. The important thing on this screen is the current state of the subscription and the action we take next: renew, change, or close.

##5. Optional: self-service and automation##
If there is time, I’ll briefly open the recurring plan settings and show the self-service options available in this database. I’ll explain which changes the customer can make through the portal, and why we should enable only the options that match our company policy.

I can also show an existing automation rule. I’ll point to its trigger and action—for example, a rule that creates a follow-up activity for a subscription with a low satisfaction rating. The main point is to see what event starts the automation and who is expected to follow up.

##6. Subscription transition##
That was the subscription workflow: we checked the recurring plan and product, created a customer quotation, confirmed it, and followed it into invoicing. We also saw where to renew or change the subscription.

Now let’s move to Helpdesk. After a customer buys a product or service, they may need support. I’ll show how Odoo turns that request into a ticket the team can track.

#1.5#Helpdesk#/#

##1. Helpdesk dashboard and teams##
Helpdesk is where the team organizes customer requests as tickets. A ticket keeps the issue, customer, owner, and progress together in one record.

ᴃᴃHelpdesk ➔ Dashboardᴃᴃ
Here we can see the Helpdesk teams. I’ll point out the teams shown on the dashboard and open the team that handles customer support.

On the team page, I’ll show the **Tickets** button to open its pipeline. Then I’ll open the team settings and point out the team members. These settings tell us who can work on tickets for this team.

The exact teams depend on the company. For our example, a Warranty team could handle damaged products, while a General Support team could handle questions about services.

##2. Create and assign a ticket##
ᴃᴃHelpdesk ➔ Team ➔ Tickets ➔ Newᴃᴃ
Let’s create a ticket for a customer whose chair arrived with a damaged leg. I’ll enter a short title that explains the issue. Then I’ll select the customer and write a brief description of what happened.

I’ll show the team and assigned person fields. These are important because the ticket needs an owner. I’ll select the Warranty team and assign an employee, then save the ticket.

Now I’ll point out the saved ticket card in the pipeline. The team can see the issue and who is responsible for following up. If the customer contacts us through a configured email alias or website contact form, Odoo can also create a ticket from that channel. I’ll show the channel configuration only if it is already available in the demo database.

##3. Move the ticket through stages##
On this Kanban screen, each column is a stage and each card is a ticket. The stages describe the team’s process.

I’ll move our ticket from **New** to **In Progress** to show that someone has started working on it. If we need more information from the customer, I can move it to a waiting stage. When the issue is resolved, I’ll move it to the solved or closed stage used by this team.

While I move the card, I’ll explain that the stage is a quick visual indicator. It helps the team see what needs attention without opening every ticket.

##4. Show the SLA on the team and ticket##
An SLA, or Service Level Agreement, is the target time for handling a ticket. It helps the company set a service expectation and helps the team see which requests need attention.

ᴃᴃHelpdesk ➔ Team Settingsᴃᴃ
I’ll open the team settings and point out the SLA option and the working-hours setting. The working hours matter because the target should reflect when the team is expected to work.

ᴃᴃHelpdesk ➔ Configuration ➔ SLA Policiesᴃᴃ
Here I’ll open a simple SLA policy. I’ll point to the team, the conditions that determine which tickets qualify, the target stage, and the time limit. The exact labels may vary slightly in our database, so I’ll explain the fields that are visible on screen.

Now I’ll return to a matching ticket and show the SLA information on the ticket. The ticket stage tells us where the work is; the SLA tells us whether we are meeting the time target. Those are related, but they answer different questions.

##5. Customer ratings and after-sales actions##
After a ticket is resolved, customer ratings can show how the support experience felt to the customer.

ᴃᴃHelpdesk ➔ Team Settings ➔ Customer Ratingsᴃᴃ
I’ll show where customer ratings are enabled for the team. If there are existing responses, I’ll open the rating view and point out the rating and any comment. This gives us direct feedback to review after closing the ticket.

Now let’s use our damaged-chair ticket to show an after-sales action.

ᴃᴃHelpdesk ➔ Open the damaged-chair ticketᴃᴃ
On the ticket, I’ll point out the after-sales buttons available to this team. For our example, I’ll choose the action that matches the customer’s situation: a **Return** if the product needs to come back, or a **Refund** if money needs to be credited back.

I’ll open the return or refund form and review the customer and order information before confirming anything. This is where Helpdesk connects the customer issue with the related sales or inventory workflow. I’ll explain that the right action depends on the company’s policy and the details of the order.

##6. Helpdesk transition##
We’ve followed one support ticket from creation to assignment, moved it through the pipeline, checked its SLA, and looked at feedback and after-sales actions.

Now I’ll move to Marketing. We have a subscription service and a support process; Marketing helps us communicate those offers and updates to the right audience.

#1.5#Marketing#/#

##1. Email Marketing dashboard##
Marketing helps us communicate with customers and potential customers. I’ll focus on email mailings, campaigns, A/B testing, and a short automation example.

ᴃᴃEmail Marketing ➔ Dashboardᴃᴃ
Here we are on the email marketing dashboard. I’ll point out the mailings shown here and the basic information visible in the list, such as the subject and status.

A **mailing** is one standalone email. A **campaign** groups related mailings into a broader sequence. For example, the first email can introduce the Furniture Care Plan, and a later email can provide a reminder. Each email is a mailing; together they can be part of one campaign.

##2. Choose the audience##
ᴃᴃEmail Marketing ➔ Mailing Lists ➔ Mailing Listsᴃᴃ
Here I’ll open the mailing lists and select the list for existing furniture customers. I’ll point out the contacts on the list and explain why this audience is relevant to the offer.

We want to send a useful message to the right people. I’ll also remind everyone that if a contact has opted out, we must respect that preference.

##3. Prepare an email mailing##
ᴃᴃEmail Marketing ➔ Mailings ➔ Newᴃᴃ
Now I’ll create a mailing called “Furniture Care Plan.” First, I’ll enter the subject. Then I’ll select the mailing list as the recipients and open the email editor.

On the editor screen, I’ll show the building blocks or template options. I’ll add a heading, a short description of the plan, and one clear button or call to action. I’ll keep the message focused so the customer can quickly understand what we are offering.

Before sending, I’ll use the preview and check the message layout, subject, recipients, and links. If available, I’ll send a test email to myself. I’ll stop before sending to the full list unless this is an approved live campaign.

If I’m demonstrating a campaign, I’ll open the campaign feature and show where related mailings are grouped. If the feature is not enabled, I’ll show its setting first. On screen, I’ll compare the campaign with an individual mailing so the difference is clear.

##4. Compare versions with A/B testing##
If we are not sure which subject line or call to action works better, we can test two versions of the mailing.

ᴃᴃEmail Marketing ➔ Open a mailing ➔ A/B Testingᴃᴃ
I’ll open the test setup and show the two versions. For example, one version can focus on convenience and the other on the service benefit. Then I’ll point to the test audience portion and the measure we want to compare, such as opens or clicks.

Odoo sends the versions to a portion of the recipients, compares the selected result, and can send the stronger version to the remaining audience. I’ll explain that the result depends on the measure we chose. More opens may not automatically mean more sales.

##5. Show a marketing automation campaign##
Marketing automation can trigger activities based on a defined audience and conditions. I’ll use an existing test campaign so we can focus on the workflow rather than building every setting from scratch.

ᴃᴃMarketing Automation ➔ Campaigns ➔ Open a test campaignᴃᴃ
On the campaign screen, I’ll point out the target audience or filter first. Then I’ll open one activity and show what it does, such as sending an email or creating a follow-up task.

I’ll show the condition that controls when the next activity happens. Then I’ll click the test option and choose a sample record. I’ll review the result to check that the right record entered the workflow and that the expected activity was created.

I’ll explain that testing lets us catch mistakes before launching a campaign to real customers. After the test, we still need to review the message, audience, timing, and customer preferences before launch.

##6. Closing##
Let’s connect what we saw on screen.

In Subscriptions, we set up a recurring product and followed it through quotation, confirmation, invoicing, and renewal. In Helpdesk, we created a ticket, assigned it to a team member, moved it through stages, checked its SLA, and reviewed after-sales actions. In Marketing, we selected an audience, prepared an email, compared versions, and tested an automation workflow.

These modules support different steps in the customer relationship, and Odoo lets us connect the related information and processes.

That’s all for my presentation. Thank you for listening. I’m happy to take your questions.
